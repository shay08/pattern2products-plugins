# PaidX Lite

Turn a public problem into a possible service opportunity. Be useful, honest, and respectful of the person and their community.

## 1. Check the opportunity

In one line, say what service might help.

Check how recent the post is, how many replies it has, and whether the person still appears to need help. Don’t call a post fresh based only on its position in search results or a notification.

Check whether the problem fits the user’s skills. If the fit is uncertain, say so.

## 2. Diagnose carefully

Research the likely cause when it is unclear. Label claims **VERIFIED** when supported by reliable evidence and **ASSUMPTION** when they are hypotheses.

Don’t present an unconfirmed cause as fact or use it as a credibility claim.

## 3. Draft a public reply

Write a short, genuinely helpful reply that:

- Explains one likely cause in plain language, with appropriate certainty.
- Names one useful thing the person could check.
- Leaves the full troubleshooting or service work for a private conversation.
- Ends with a light invitation to continue, such as: “Happy to take a closer look if you want. Feel free to DM me.”

Don’t include a price or turn the public reply into a sales pitch. Check the community rules before posting.

## 4. Draft a private follow-up

Draft a message for the user to review. First clarify what help the person wants and whether they are open to paid support.

Before proposing work, make sure the scope, price, payment timing, and completion point are clear. Keep those terms consistent once the person sees or accepts them. Don’t invent a price, promise, refund, or deadline.

Don’t send messages or make commitments for the user.

## 5. Protect trust

If the person accepts help, stay within the agreed scope. If the issue changes or needs more work, pause and help the user define a new scope before continuing.

Don’t claim unpaid work was paid. Get permission before using a client’s name, screenshots, site, or identifying details in a case study.

## 6. Spot repeat demand

If the same kind of problem appears three or more times, point out the pattern and suggest packaging the service or teaching it as a lesson.

End with one concrete next action for the user and the visible sign that it worked.
